The flood problem

One release, two shorelines

When Lake Ontario runs high, releasing more water can spare its shoreline. That water travels down the St. Lawrence to Lake St. Louis near Montréal, where the Ottawa River also arrives. Can the two operators make better use of the room downstream by planning together?

What is Plan 2014?

Plan 2014 is the International Joint Commission’s plan for regulating Lake Ontario outflow through the Moses–Saunders Dam. It sets weekly releases using lake levels, water supplies and conditions along the river. How the regulation board sets outflows

Our baseline, filtered Plan 2014, starts from the simulator’s rule-curve proposal. It passes through the native release limits and the same Ottawa feasibility check as the two model predictive controllers (MPCs).

Where the rivers meet

In the study, each Ottawa release takes one quarter-month to reach Lake St. Louis. Water already on its way at the start of the year counts toward the first arrival.

Inside the simulation

Simulator
The Lake Ontario–St. Lawrence River regulation simulator supplies the lake balance, release limits and downstream water levels. The team adds a synthetic Ottawa reservoir and two MPCs. Original simulator · Coupled study · Project technology
Clock
Four decisions per month, 48 per year. Each operator executes the first planned release, observes the next state and replans. April through June spans steps 13–24.
Competing objectives
High water threatens both shorelines. Low water affects recreational boating, and flow through turbines supplies hydropower. The study compares flood exposure, affected buildings, boating costs and hydropower value.
Hard limits in these runs
Releases must satisfy the native Lake Ontario rules, Ottawa storage and outlet bounds, and a combined downstream flow ceiling. Lake Ontario levels above 75.50 m incur a cost. The team’s next comparison calls for both MPCs to enforce forecast stage limits at both shorelines.
